## Results and Discussion
I ran the timing benchmark for the GPU implementation on larger variant windows then for the CPU, numpy, and naive approaches. Namely, as seen in benchmark_gpu.py, window sizes of [5000, 8000, 10000, 15000, 20000, 30000, 40000] - still at 600 samples. Figure 2 shows a smooth increase from 5000 to 20000 variants, then a sharp spike in runtime from 20000 to 30000 variants: elapsed time rose from 0.038s to 0.370s. Figure 2 is created without the .cpu() transfer_out step; Figures 3 and 4 are created with the transfer_out step in place. Figures 3 and 4 are aimed to show the discrepancy in transfer_in and transfer_out times in relation to the compute time. 

To investigate further, I produced the decomposed time for the process, separating the total runtime of ld_gpu to transfer_in_time, compute_time, and transfer_out_time. transfer_in_time corresponds the G.to(device) step; compute_time corresponds to the matmul calculation; and transfer_out_time corresponds to the matrix.cpu() - transfer back to cpu time. Observing figure 3, transfer_out_time and transfer_in_time both show a smooth increasing curve across the full variant window (from 5000 to 40000 variants), but compute_time has a spike from 20000 to 30000 variants, mirroring the spike in figure 2 of the total runtime on this exact interval. In figure 3, transfer_out_time is consistently the most time-consuming step only until the last and largest variant window: this is because the PCIe connection between the GPU and CPU becomes the bottleneck when transferring a large n by n LD matrix post computation. The transfer_in_time, on the other hand, remains low compared to the transfer_out_time because the input matrix, G, is n by m, where m is the number of samples - a constant value of 600 in this experiment. Thus, the PCIe bandwidth of roughly 25 GB/s bottlenecks the run: in figure 4, the transfer_out_time accounts for roughly 50% of the total time at 40000 with a quadratic scaling LD matrix whilst the input time becomes negligible as it scales linearly with variant count. 

I ran a GPU usage log using nvidia-smi on my 4070Ti - it showed a peak VRAM reservation for PyTorch of 11793 MiB of the available 12288 MiB on the card. Tensor operations allocate memory per operation from the VRAM and it is reserved perpetually until explicitly cleared, as shown in the peak VRAM allocation which persists. The LD computation requires two steps, a matmul $tmp = G_std @ G_std.T$ and a division $tmp/(m-1)$, allocating two n by n matrices with the second step reading from the matmul matrix and writing onto another matrix. This quickly takes up VRAM at large-n because of the quadratic growth rate of the n by n matrix. 

Near the VRAM ceiling, when free memory is fragmented because of long-lived data persisting and short-lived data clearing out, there is a lack of a contiguous block of memory. Then, PyTorch falls back to 'cudaMalloc', which carves out GPU memory for calculation - if it still doesn't work, PyTorch then is forced into a memory-freeing and retry cycle; this cycle can be unpredictable and may account for the spike in compute_time around 30000 variants. 

For almost every window size (from 5k to 30k), the bottleneck in computation becomes transferring data back from the GPU to CPU over the PCIe, not the compute; however, at the largest window size of 40k, the inefficiency in memory usage of the two separate n by n allocations pushes VRAM to become the leading constraint.
